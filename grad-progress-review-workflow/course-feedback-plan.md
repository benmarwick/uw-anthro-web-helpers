# Plan: Instructor Feedback on Graduate Students in Courses (UW Anthropology)

*Drafted 16 November 2026. This is a plan only; nothing has been built yet. It is a companion to [`grad-annual-review-plan.md`](grad-annual-review-plan.md) and uses its role names: the **GPC** is the Graduate Program Coordinator (faculty), the **Graduate Student Administrator** is the staff member who runs the cycle.*

## 1. Summary

At the end of every quarter, each instructor who had one of the department's graduate students in a course gets **one link** to a short page listing all of those students. For each student the instructor marks whether they had enough contact to comment. If they did, the instructor gives three quick stage-relative ratings, names one strength, and gives one next step. A student the instructor cannot comment on takes about 30 seconds, and one with comments takes 3–4 minutes (§5.2).

Each quarter, the GPC checks the comments and releases them to the student one to two weeks after the page closes, while the course is still recent. Once a year the system compiles every student's feedback into a **Course Feedback Record**. The GPC reads it, adds a short synthesis if useful, and releases it to the student. The record then feeds that student's annual review. Each subfield coordinator receives the records of the students in their subfield, in the calibration view, for review by that subfield's faculty (§6.4).

**Patterns across these programs:**

- **How instructor views are gathered.** By a written report per course (Emory, Boulder, MSU, Cincinnati, Brown, Rochester, UC Irvine, Ohio State and Northwestern Philosophy), by instructors speaking at the faculty meeting (Buffalo, USC, Pittsburgh and Florida Anthropology), or by the student collecting the comments already written on their work (UCSD, UC Davis).
- **Timing.** Most collect at the end of every term. Cincinnati collects before each of its two yearly reviews, and UC Davis once, at the end of year 1.
- **Scope.** Many limit collection to early-stage students: MSU and Buffalo cover years 1–2; UC Irvine, UC Davis and UCSD cover year 1; Emory covers core and anthropology elective courses; UW Philosophy runs until the qualifying paper.
- **Prompts.** Nearly every form asks for strengths and weaknesses. MSU also asks about the student's "demeanor" and "interpersonal skills," which are attributes of the person; this plan asks only about the work (§2.3).
- **Scales.** Short scales of 3 to 5 points.
- **Stage-relative wording.** Several anchor judgements to the student's stage: "for his/her stage" (Miami), "readiness for independent doctoral research" (Penn), "foundation for a qualifying paper" (UW Philosophy), "ability to complete doctoral work" (Rochester).
- **Lower burden.** Several programs reuse comments instructors have already written: UW Philosophy, Pittsburgh HAA, Penn History, UCSD and UC Davis.
- **Transparency.** Where a written report exists, the student usually sees it verbatim and attributed. Emory and Rochester state that these reports differ from confidential letters of recommendation. Where instructors speak at a meeting, the student gets only a summary letter.
- **Stakes.** Rochester uses the reports to decide "which students shall or shall not continue." UC Davis rates the first-year portfolio Satisfactory or Unsatisfactory.
- **No concern flag.** None of the forms read has a concern checkbox. Boulder's mid-year meeting reviews "students whose performance has raised faculty concerns," but no source describes a way for one instructor to raise a concern during the term.

**What this plan adds.** Compiling instructor reports for the student is established practice. Cincinnati Philosophy compiles each term's reports into a single letter twice a year, and Brown Comparative Literature and Brown English send a letter from the Director of Graduate Studies and give students the faculty's written evaluations. Boulder, Pittsburgh, USC and Florida summarize instructor views in a letter. None of the sources read combines these elements:

- one page per instructor covering all of their graduate students;
- a one-click "not enough contact" answer;
- fixed, stage-relative ratings with a single strength and a single next step;
- a year-long record that gathers every next step into one list, with a short synthesis by the GPC;
- a route for an instructor to raise a concern within the quarter.

### 2.3 Evidence on brief narrative feedback

Each row gives what the source reports, checked against its abstract or full text on 17 November 2026, and what it implies for this design. Effect sizes keep the sample sizes and intervals the sources report.

**What kind of feedback helps**

| Finding | Source | Design consequence |
|------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| Across 607 effect sizes (23,663 observations), feedback improved performance on average (d = .41), but more than a third of feedback interventions made it worse. Effectiveness "decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy closer to the self and away from the task" | [Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) | Both narrative fields ask about the course work. Nothing asks about the person |
| A later meta-analysis (435 studies, 994 effects, N > 61,000) found d = 0.48 overall, with large heterogeneity. Feedback type mattered most: "high-information" feedback, which tells the learner what went wrong, why, and what to do next, had d = 0.99 (95% CI 0.82 to 1.15; 42 effects), against 0.46 for corrective feedback and 0.24 for reinforcement or punishment | [Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087) | The strength asks for an example, and the next step asks what to do and how. Together they approximate high-information feedback within two 500-character fields |
| In a college course (N = 464), detailed feedback on an essay was "strongly related to student improvement," and was "most effective when given alone, unaccompanied by grades or praise"; a tentative grade "depressed performance." Among fifth- and sixth-graders, comments alone produced the highest interest and performance, while grades and grades plus comments had "similar and generally undermining effects" | [Lipnevich & Smith 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017841); [Butler 1988](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1988.tb00874.x) | The form collects no grade and no overall score, and the record computes no average (principles 4 and 6) |
| In two studies of written peer feedback (N = 20 and N = 350), medical students believed "feedback sandwiches" (praise, critique, praise) improved their later work, but there was no evidence that they did | [Parkes, Abercrombie & McCarty 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10459-012-9377-9) | The strength field exists to tell the student what to keep doing. Instructors need not wrap the next step in praise |
| In applied classroom studies, immediate feedback was usually more effective than delayed feedback; laboratory studies gave mixed or opposite results (53 studies). A review of formative feedback recommends that it be "nonevaluative, supportive, timely, and specific" | [Kulik & Kulik 1988](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543058001079); [Shute 2008](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313795) | Releasing feedback once a year means autumn comments reach the student about four months after the course ends, and spring comments about ten months after. This is the main evidence for releasing each quarter's comments one to two weeks after the page closes (decision 2, §6.2), with the yearly record kept. The follow-up flag already reaches the student within the quarter (§5.3) |
| Feedback changes behavior only if the learner engages with it. A review of 195 publications classified interventions by the "recipience processes" they target, and a conceptual paper names four parts of student "feedback literacy": appreciating feedback, making judgments, managing affect, and taking action | [Winstone et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2016.1207538); [Carless & Boud 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2018.1463354) | In the self-report, the student names the one or two next steps they will act on and how; the advisor discusses it and ticks "Discussed course feedback" (§6.1, §8) |

| Finding | Source | Design consequence |
|------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|

## 3. Design principles

1.  **One link per instructor per quarter.** All of that instructor's graduate students in a course appear on one page. Multi-section and joint-listed offerings are merged, so nobody gets duplicates.
2.  **"Not enough contact" is a respectable one-click answer.** A student in a 150-seat lecture may be invisible to the instructor. Forcing a comment produces noise.
3.  **Short, prompted narrative.** The narrative is two capped fields with fixed prompts, a strength and a next step. The form takes no pasted text and no free-for-all box: more boxes gave a smaller share of constructive comments (Hauer et al. 2018), and capped fields with instructions are consistent with the letter evidence (Miller et al. 2019, §2.3).
4.  **About the course work only.** The form collects no grades: the transcript has them, and UW Philosophy notes that grades cannot be emailed. It collects nothing on TA or RA performance, which stays in the TA system under UAW Article 22.1 (principle 8 of the annual review plan). It does not ask about personality, health or personal circumstances.
5.  **Attributed and visible to the student.** Every comment carries the instructor's name and course. Nothing is anonymous, and the form says so. This is what FERPA requires anyway (§9), and peers do the same.
6.  **Low-stakes points, one considered record.** Single comments are never averaged, ranked or used alone for any decision. Rating levels are read within one student's record and never compared across instructors or students: signed ratings that the student will read probably skew high, and instructors differ in how they use a scale (§2.3).
7.  **Concerns do not wait for June.** An instructor can ask the GPC to follow up this quarter (§5.3).
8.  **No fellow students.** Graduate students who are instructors of record, and quiz-section TAs, are never asked (Policy 3.7.2: "Fellow students should not be involved").
9.  **One system.** This is a module of the annual review system, not a third app. It uses the same `Students` roster, legal basis, retention series, recusal rules and infrastructure, and the GPC owns and maintains it (annual review plan principle 4).
10. **Feedback reaches the student while the course is recent.** Each quarter's comments are released one to two weeks after the page closes (§6.2). The classroom evidence favours timely feedback (Kulik & Kulik 1988; Shute 2008, §2.3), and a once-a-year release would reach the student four to ten months after the course.

## 5. The instructor's page

### 5.1 Invitation

The email is short and holds one link and no student content. Following Registrar guidance, the email names no student and includes no grade or comment.

> *Subject: Brief feedback on graduate students in ANTH 512 (Winter 2027)* You had 3 Anthropology graduate students in ANTH 512 this quarter. **Shown at the top of the page, in three lines:**

- Describe the work, not the person: what they did and what they should do next.
- The student will read this with your name on it. It is part of their education record.
- Don't include grades, TA work, or health or personal circumstances. If those matter, tick "follow up" and talk to the GPC.

**Deliberately not asked:** an overall rating, a numeric score, a ranking against peers, a grade, a free-for-all "other comments" box, pasted final-paper comments, or a judgement of milestone potential. A milestone judgement would add a third narrative box (Hauer et al. 2018). **Burden estimate:** about 30 seconds for a "not enough contact" student and 3–4 minutes with comments. An instructor with three graduate students in a seminar spends about 10 minutes. The pilot measures this against that target.

### 5.3 Follow-up flag: urgent concerns

- The note prompt asks "What did you observe, and when?" (for example, missed submissions, or a pattern across the written work). Supervisors who judged a trainee unsatisfactory often did not report it, partly from "lack of knowledge of what to specifically document" (Dudek, Marks & Regehr 2005, §2.3).
- The GPC and the advisor get a content-free email the next day, with a link to the note.
- The GPC, or the advisor if the GPC delegates, **talks with the student within three weeks** and shares the note. The student hears about the concern then, not at the next quarterly release. A Followups screen lists open flags with the days remaining; the handler gets a reminder at two weeks, and an overdue flag shows in red on the GPC's dashboard.
- The GPC records the outcome: "Discussed," "Support arranged," or "Raised as an interim check-in." Choosing an interim check-in creates an `INTERIM` row in the annual review system. A person makes that choice; the flag never creates the row by itself (principle 2 of the annual review plan).
- The note and its outcome appear in the year's record.
- When the GPC is the student's advisor, the alternate reviewer handles the flag (recusal).

## 6. The Course Feedback Record (the GPC's collation)

### 6.1 Contents (one per student per feedback year)

The record compiles comments the student has already seen quarter by quarter (§6.2), and adds the cross-course view.

1.  **Coverage line:** for example, "6 courses; feedback from 5 instructors; 1 'not enough contact'; 0 not received." A record with fewer than two comments says, in plain words, that it is too thin to show patterns.
2.  **Ratings grid:** courses as rows, the three dimensions as columns, each cell showing the level given. **Not averaged** (principle 6).
3.  **Comments,** verbatim, attributed and in date order. For each course: the strength and the next step.
4.  **All the next steps gathered into one list**, so the student sees recurring themes.
5.  **Follow-up flags** raised that year and their recorded outcomes.
6.  **GPC synthesis** (optional): 2–4 sentences on patterns across courses, written by the GPC with no AI drafting, consistent with the repository's practice for decisions about students.
7.  **Student response:** in a new field of the annual review self-report, the student names the one or two next steps they will act on this year, and how (§8). Feedback changes behavior only if the learner engages with it and takes action (Winstone et al. 2017; Carless & Boud 2018, §2.3). The advisor discusses the answer at the annual meeting.

### 6.2 The GPC's steps

**Each quarter, starting the week after the page closes (about 2 minutes per comment):**

1.  Open the batch view of that quarter's comments, filtered by subfield.

2.  **Read each comment before release**, with a four-item check on screen:

    - Is anything about the person rather than the work?
    - Is anything outside course work (grades, TA work, health)?
    - Is any comment unclear?
    - Is anything faint praise or hedged, so that the student could not tell what the instructor means? Readers judge applicants lower when letters contain such "doubt raisers," and letter writers use them more for women (Madera et al. 2019, §2.3).

    The GPC **never edits an instructor's words.** If a comment needs to change, the GPC returns it to the instructor with a logged reason, and the instructor revises it within 5 days. If the instructor has not revised it by release, the comment goes **to the student only**, who is entitled to it, with a GPC note. It is kept out of the advisor's view, the calibration view and the PDF copies until the instructor revises it or the GPC resolves it.

3.  **Release the quarter's comments.** The student and the advisor(s) see them in the app (§6.4), and the student gets a content-free email saying new feedback is available.

The Winter schedule is tight. Its page closes Fri 2 Apr and its comments are released Fri 9 Apr, so a returned comment can be revised in time only if the GPC returns it by Mon 5 Apr. Otherwise the rule for unrevised comments above applies.

**Once a year, in spring week 2 (about 5 minutes per student):**

4.  Open the batch view of draft records. Each compiles the comments already released in the feedback year, plus the Winter comments released the same day.
5.  Add the synthesis if useful. Note where next steps from different instructors conflict, since struggling women residents received contradictory feedback more often than men (Mueller et al. 2017, §2.3).
6.  **Release the record.** It appears in the student's annual review form (`STUDENT_PENDING`) and in the advisor's view before their meeting.

For the GPC's own advisees, the alternate reviewer (by default the department chair) does steps 1–6.

### 6.3 Students without a spring annual review

Every student gets their feedback, whether or not they have a spring review.

- **MA (Archaeological Heritage) students.** They see each quarter's comments with the quarterly release, like everyone else, and the comments are also shown at their quarterly `INTERIM` check-in (annual review plan §4.4). Their programme is 3–4 quarters, so a yearly record would arrive too late.
- **Students marked `ON_LEAVE` or `EXEMPT` for the spring review** who had courses in the feedback year. The record is released on its own, in the app and as a PDF, on the same day as everyone else's.
- **Students leaving the program** through graduation, withdrawal or transfer. A **closing record** compiles every quarter since the last yearly record, including a final quarter whose comments were already released. It is released when the administrator marks the student as leaving in `Students`.

### 6.4 Where the comments and the record go

Each quarter's comments, at release, go only to the student and the advisor(s). Everyone else in the table sees comments only through the yearly record.

|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| Student | Each quarter's comments at release, then the full record in the app, plus a PDF shared only with their UW account |

No free text unless the GPC opens it for a specific student (decision 7). A coordinator who is also the student's advisor sees the full record in that role |

## 9. Privacy and legal basis

- **Education records.** UW's Registrar says notes stop being "sole possession" once others can inspect them: "they are no longer sole possession and become education records. Students have the right to inspect and review those records" ([FERPA for faculty and staff](https://registrar.washington.edu/staff-faculty/ferpa/)). Every field, including flags and returned comments, is therefore visible to the student on request, and the plan simply shows it to them. A FERPA waiver covers only confidential recommendations for admission, employment or honors (34 CFR 99.12), so it cannot shield course feedback.
- **Email.** Emails carry no student-specific content. The Registrar warns against grades in email and advises NetID-authenticated sharing "especially when it involves more than one student record." Extending this to evaluative comments is our inference, and a cautious one.
- **Separate from employment.** Feedback covers enrollment in courses, not ASE work. TA and RA evaluations stay in the TA system.
- **Roster data.** From MyGrad, the Roster page receives only each student's UW email and the target quarter's curriculum, course number and section; from the Time Schedule, it receives section details. The page stores nothing in the browser. `Enrollments` keeps the rows under the same retention as the review (§8).
- **Privacy notice:** add a course-feedback section to the annual review's notice, covering what instructors submit, who sees it, the student's rights to inspect and request amendment (WAC 478-140), and retention.

## 11. Decisions

Settled on 17 November 2026, each at its recommended default. For decision 8, the pilot uses the labels alone, and the level descriptions are revisited after the pilot debrief. The graduate committee can revisit any decision.

| \# | Decision | Recommended default |
|------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| 1 | Which quarters does one record cover, and when is it released? | **Spring + Autumn + Winter, released at the end of spring week 2** so it feeds that year's review. The alternative is a June digest of Autumn–Spring, which reaches the review a year late |
| 2 | Should students also see each quarter's feedback as soon as its page closes? | **Yes: release each quarter's comments after the GPC's check, one to two weeks after the page closes (§6.2), and keep the yearly record.** The classroom evidence favours timely feedback (Kulik & Kulik 1988; Shute 2008), though no study tests delays of months. UW Philosophy, Northwestern, MSU and Architecture also give feedback straight to the student. Costs: the GPC checks comments three times a year, and the spring release falls in July. The alternative is a year-end release only, which reaches the student four to ten months after the course. The Winter 2027 pilot releases on the same day either way |
| 3 | Which students are covered? | **All students in the roster.** UW Philosophy and MSU stop at an early milestone; ours limits itself because later students take few courses |
| 4 | Ask instructors outside the department? | **Yes, from autumn 2027** |
| 5 | Ask the advisor about their own advisee in a course? | **Yes, with "Covered in my annual review" as one click** |
| 6 | Append the record to the annual review PDF? | **Yes**, so the committee chair sees it |
| 7 | Show free-text comments at subfield calibration? | **No.** Coverage, grid and flags only; the GPC can open more for a specific student |

| 10 | Allow instructors to edit after submitting? | **Yes, until the page closes.** After that, only through the GPC's "return for revision" route |

## 12. Risks

- **Roster gaps.** If the MyGrad or Time Schedule step fails, coverage will be patchy. The CSV upload fallback (§4.5) and the BI Portal cross-check (§4.2) reduce this, and the coverage line makes gaps visible rather than hiding them.

- **Page redesigns.** A redesign of MyGrad or the Time Schedule would break the bookmarklets. The MyGrad bookmarklet checks that each page names the student it asked for, which turns some breakage into a visible failure, and the import reports unmatched rows. The administrator reruns both steps after any visible change to either site.

- **Instructor fatigue.** Keep the page to one screen, keep "not enough contact" to one click, cap reminders at two, and measure minutes in the pilot. The only optional field left is the follow-up flag, so if the burden is high, the graduate committee decides which rating to drop; the strength and next step stay.

- **Bland or hedged comments.** Expected in year 1 (Ginsburg). The fixed strength and next-step prompts and the follow-up route are the main remedies. The GPC's "return for revision" route is for comments the student could not act on, including faint praise and hedging (§6.2), not for style.

- **Ratings shown beside comments.** In two studies, grades given with comments undermined the comments' effect (Lipnevich & Smith 2009; Butler 1988). The three stage-relative ratings resemble grades, and the student sees them with the comments. No cited study tests whether showing the comments first, or hiding the ratings from the student, would help, and FERPA gives the student access to the ratings anyway (§9). The pilot debrief asks students whether the ratings or the comments shaped what they did next.

- **Build capacity.** This module and the annual review are both built in Jan–Feb 2027 (annual review plan §14). The department chose on 17 November 2026 to keep both on schedule. If the build slips, this module's pilot moves first, to Spring 2027 courses (pages open 7 June), and its first record then covers Spring alone.

- **Over-reading one comment.** The design has no averages. The thin-record wording and the calibration view showing no free text by default guard against it.
